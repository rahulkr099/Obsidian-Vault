# 1. What are Business Rules?

A **Business Rule** is a statement that defines **what is allowed, required, or prohibited** in the system according to the business.

Business rules answer questions like:

- Who can perform an action?
    
- Under what conditions is an action allowed?
    
- What validations must always be true?
    
- What should happen if a rule is violated?
    

These rules ensure the application behaves according to business policies.

---

## Simple Definition

Think of a business rule as:

> **A rule the business wants every user to follow.**

Example:

```text
Only the author of a blog post
can edit that post.
```

That rule should always be enforced.

---

# 2. Why are Business Rules Important?

Imagine you have this API:

```http
PATCH /posts/:id
```

Without business rules:

Anyone could edit anyone else's post.

With business rules:

```text
User owns the post?

↓

Yes → Allow update

↓

No → Return 403 Forbidden
```

Business rules protect data integrity and user trust.

---

# 3. Functional Requirement vs Business Rule

Many beginners confuse these.

### Functional Requirement

Describes **what the system should do**.

Example:

```text
The system shall allow
authors to edit blog posts.
```

---

### Business Rule

Describes **when and under what conditions** it is allowed.

Example:

```text
Only the author
who created the post
may edit it.
```

---

Comparison:

|Functional Requirement|Business Rule|
|---|---|
|Describes a feature|Defines conditions for that feature|
|"User can publish posts."|"Only verified authors can publish."|
|"User can comment."|"Cannot comment on deleted posts."|

---

# 4. Characteristics of Good Business Rules

A good business rule should be:

- Clear
    
- Specific
    
- Testable
    
- Consistent
    
- Independent of technology
    

Bad:

```text
Users should behave responsibly.
```

Good:

```text
A user may like
a post only once.
```

# 5. Types of Business Rules

Business rules often fall into these categories.

---

## A. Permission Rules

Define **who** can perform an action.

Example:

```text
Only administrators
can delete users.
```

---

```text
Only authors
can publish posts.
```

---

## B. Validation Rules

Define what data is considered valid.

Example:

```text
Email addresses
must be unique.
```

---

```text
Password
must contain
at least eight characters.
```

---

## C. Process Rules

Define the order of operations.

Example:

```text
User must verify email

↓

Then

↓

Can log in
```

---

## D. Constraint Rules

Define limits.

Example:

```text
Maximum upload size

↓

10 MB
```

---

```text
Maximum 5 tags
per post.
```

---

## E. Calculation Rules

Define how values are computed.

Example:

```text
Total Price

=

Item Price

+

Tax

-

Discount
```

# 7. Business Rules Drive Service Layer Logic

Suppose the API is:

```http
PATCH /posts/:id/publish
```

The service layer might follow this logic:

```text
Receive Request

↓

Find Post

↓

Does Post Exist?

↓

No → 404

↓

Yes

↓

Is User Owner?

↓

No → 403

↓

Yes

↓

Has Title?

↓

No → Validation Error

↓

Has Content?

↓

No → Validation Error

↓

Publish Post

↓

Return Success
```

Notice:

The controller doesn't decide these rules.

The **service layer** does.

# 11. Business Rules and Testing


# 15. Where Should Business Rules Live?

Professional layered architecture:

```text
Controller

↓

Service Layer

↓

Repository

↓

Database
```

### Controller

- Receive request
    
- Validate request format
    
- Call service
    

---

### Service

- Apply business rules
    
- Make decisions
    
- Coordinate operations
    

---

### Repository

- Read/write data
    
- No business decisions
    

This separation keeps code clean and maintainable.

# 16. Common Mistakes

## Mistake 1

Putting business rules inside controllers.

Wrong:

```javascript
if (user.role !== "AUTHOR") {
    return res.status(403);
}
```

Controllers should stay thin.

Move the decision into the service layer.

---

## Mistake 2

Duplicating business rules.

Don't repeat the same rule in multiple places.

Keep a single source of truth.

---

## Mistake 3

Mixing validation with business rules.

Example:

```text
Email format valid
```

This is input validation.

---

Example:

```text
Email must be unique
```

This is a business rule.

---

## Mistake 4

Hardcoding numbers.

Bad:

```text
Maximum upload

↓

10 MB
```

Scattering this value throughout the code.

Better:

Use configuration or constants.

---

## Mistake 5

Not documenting business rules.

Undocumented rules are easily forgotten and inconsistently implemented.

---

# 17. Business Rules Checklist

For every feature, ask:

- Who is allowed to perform this action?
    
- What conditions must be true?
    
- What conditions make the action invalid?
    
- What happens if the rule is broken?
    
- Which layer should enforce this rule?
    
- How will we test it?

# Interview Questions

### Q1. What is a business rule?

**Answer:**

A business rule is a statement that defines the conditions, constraints, or policies that govern how a system should behave according to business requirements.

---

### Q2. How is a business rule different from a functional requirement?

**Answer:**

A functional requirement describes what the system should do, while a business rule defines the conditions under which that functionality is allowed or restricted.

---

### Q3. Where should business rules be implemented in a layered backend architecture?

**Answer:**

Business rules should primarily be implemented in the **service layer**, which contains the application's business logic. Controllers should handle HTTP concerns, and repositories should handle data access.

---

### Q4. Give examples of common business rules.

**Answer:**

- Only the owner can edit their profile.
    
- One user can like a post only once.
    
- Email addresses must be unique.
    
- Only verified users can publish content.
    
- Expired links cannot be accessed.

# Key Takeaways

- Business rules define the **policies and constraints** of the application.
    
- They answer questions like **who**, **when**, and **under what conditions**.
    
- The **service layer** is the primary home for business rules.
    
- Business rules influence validation, authorization, database design, APIs, and testing.
    
- Documenting business rules makes your backend more consistent and easier to maintain.
    

---

# Real-World Backend Perspective

In many companies, when a Product Manager says:

> "Users should only be able to edit their own posts."

they are **not** describing an API—they are describing a **business rule**.

As a backend engineer, your responsibility is to translate that rule into:

- Service-layer logic
    
- Authorization checks
    
- Database constraints (if applicable)
    
- Test cases
    
- Error responses
    

This is why experienced backend engineers spend significant time understanding business rules before writing code.
